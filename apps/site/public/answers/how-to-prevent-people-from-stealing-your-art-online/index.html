How to prevent people from stealing your art online

Short answerYou cannot fully prevent copying once art is public, but you can make theft harder and easier to prove. Post smaller files, keep dated originals, put your name in the file, register important work with your copyright office, and know the takedown route before you need it.

Make the original easy to prove

In a dispute, the person who can show the work in progress usually wins. Keep layered files, sketches, raw photos and exports with their dates. Post a lower-resolution version publicly and keep the full-resolution file to yourself.

Put your name in the file's metadata and, if it suits the piece, a small mark in a corner. A mark that sits across the image is harder to crop out than one in a corner but changes how the work looks, so it is your call.

Register work that matters

In the United States, registration is generally required before you can sue for infringement (17 U.S.C. 411), and statutory damages and attorney's fees are generally unavailable for infringement that began before registration unless an exception applies, such as registering within three months of first publication (17 U.S.C. 412). Rules differ in other countries and this is not legal advice. Confirm the details with your copyright office.

Consider protection tools, with honest limits

Glaze and Nightshade, from the University of Chicago's SAND Lab, add changes to images meant to disrupt how AI models learn an artist's style. Researchers and reviewers note limits: the changes can be visible on flat-color art, they are not future-proof, and they do nothing to stop a person from reposting your file. Confirm current details on the project page.

Know where your work shows up

Run a reverse image search on your best-known pieces every so often, using Google search by image or TinEye. Early discovery makes a takedown simpler. If you find a copy, see what to do if someone steals your artwork.

People also ask

Does a watermark stop theft?

No. It makes casual reposting less appealing and helps you prove origin, but a determined person can crop or edit it out.

Is right-click protection worth it?

No. Anything shown on a screen can be saved, so it only annoys honest visitors.

Do I need to register my art?

Not to own it, but in the US registration is generally needed before you can sue, and timing affects damages. Check your own country's rules.

Will Glaze or Nightshade stop all AI training?

No. They are described by their makers and independent reviewers as a partial measure that may weaken over time.
